# Meta Levels and the Interview

Meta software engineer levels run from E3 to E10, and E5 is the senior level. Meta interviews each candidate against a target level, and the loop is scored against that level's expectations. Below E5 the loop centers on coding. From E5 upward, design and behavioral rounds carry real weight.

These levels are commonly reported by employees and candidates. They are not an official Meta publication.

## The ladder

| Level | Title | Typical scope |
|---|---|---|
| **E3** | Software Engineer (entry) | Well-defined tasks inside one team, with regular review |
| **E4** | Software Engineer | Features built end to end with little supervision |
| **E5** | Senior Software Engineer | A project or problem area owned for one team |
| **E6** | Staff Software Engineer | Work that spans several teams |
| **E7** | Senior Staff Software Engineer | Technical direction for an organization of many teams |
| **E8** | Principal Engineer | Direction across a whole product line or division |
| **E9** | Distinguished Engineer | Company-wide technical direction |
| **E10** | Engineering Fellow | Company-wide and industry-wide influence |

The E ladder covers individual contributors, who are engineers without direct reports. Managers are on a parallel M ladder, and a first-line manager (M1) is reported to carry about the same scope as E5. Meta is reported not to hire directly into E10.

## Why E5 matters

E5 is the terminal level, which means an engineer can hold it for a whole career without pressure to be promoted. Below E5, Meta expects steady promotion, a practice often called the up-or-out rule. Engineers are reported to have about five years to move from E3 to E5.

The rule gives the hiring level a lasting effect. A candidate hired at E4 must show E5 scope within a limited time. A candidate hired at E5 has no such limit.

## How the loop changes by level

| Target level | Coding | Design | Behavioral |
|---|---|---|---|
| E3 | The main evidence; speed and correctness | Usually none | Motivation and teamwork |
| E4 | The main evidence; two problems per round | Reports vary; ask the recruiter | Independent delivery of features |
| E5 | Must be passed at the same pace | One or two rounds with real weight | End-to-end ownership of a problem |
| E6 | Must be passed; rarely decides the level | Depth, trade-offs, and direction across systems | Influence across several teams |
| E7 and above | Must be passed | Direction for an organization, planned two to three years ahead | Programs led across many teams |

## How the level is decided

- The recruiter states a target level before the loop. Ask for it in the first call.
- Interviewers compare the evidence with the scope in the ladder table.
- The design round supplies most of the level evidence. Senior candidates are reported to fail on design more often than on coding.
- An offer one level below the target is called down-leveling. It usually follows senior-level coding combined with weak system design.
- Behavioral stories must match the target scope. A story about one feature supports E4, and a story about several teams supports E6.

## Practical points for candidates

1. Prepare the design round at the target level, because it is the best protection against a lower offer.
2. Write stories at the right scope before the loop: ownership for E5, influence across teams for E6.
3. Compare levels across companies with care. Meta E5 is commonly mapped to Google L5 and Apple ICT4, and every mapping is approximate.
4. Negotiate the level before the package. A competing offer at the same level is the most common argument.
5. Discuss stock separately from base pay, because the first stock grant is reported to be the most flexible part.
